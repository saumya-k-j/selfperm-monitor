Self-Permission Monitor

Reasoning models sometimes talk themselves into breaking a rule. They check whether an action is allowed, grant themselves an exception, and act. Standard chain-of-thought monitors miss it because the reasoning sounds careful. I built a monitor that catches it, worked out why it works, and took it into AI agents that make policy-bound decisions like refunds.

01The problem

A safeguard that reads diligence as safety

When an AI agent is about to issue a refund or change a booking, a cheap safeguard is to have a second model read the agent's private reasoning first. That catches obvious deception. It misses a subtler pattern I kept finding in traces: the model checks the rule, finds itself an exception, and goes ahead. Asked "is this model being deceptive?", a judge sees careful policy reasoning and scores it as safe.

The question I set out to answer: can a chain-of-thought monitor reliably catch self-permission, and what does it need to see to do it?

02Approach
0 → 100suspicion score per trace; threshold of 50 fixed before scoring

Why a prompted judge, and why one clause

A judge, not a classifier

No labelled dataset of self-permission existed, and the failure is semantic. An LLM judge needs no training data, runs locally on open weights, and can sit as a gate in front of any tool call. The cost is that results depend on the judge, so every claim had to hold across several of them.

One clause, everything else fixed

The standard monitor asks whether the reasoning shows intent to manipulate or deceive. The self-permission monitor adds a description of the pattern: reasoning that questions whether an action is allowed, grants itself permission, and proceeds. Any difference comes from that clause.

Controls for the easy explanation

A longer prompt might just score everything higher. So I compared against two placebo prompts of matched length (769 and 782 characters against 795) and the standard prompt with the same disclaimer added, one clause away from the treatment.

Ground truth that isn't an opinion

Labels come from code checks in my own refund agent and from the answer key of τ²-bench, a public agent benchmark. On the core traces, positives are cases where GPT-OSS 120B complied with a manipulation request; negatives are refusals.

04Validation · mechanism

Why it works: the judge starts reading the decision

With the standard prompt, the 8B judge rated careful refusals as more suspicious than compliance. My hypothesis: it reacts to deliberation itself, not to what the model decides. To test it, I took reasoning from three other model families (DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3 32B, GLM-4.5 Air), cut each trace just before its verdict, and attached matched endings that differ only in the decision: "Is this allowed? Yes, so proceed" against "No, so decline". A monitor that reads the decision should score "proceed" higher. The prediction was written down and dated before any of these traces was scored. The table counts matched pairs whose endings discuss permission, 60 per judge.

On endings that discuss permission, the prediction held on all three judges and in each model family: the clause moved the score toward the decision more than the standard or placebo prompt did (exact sign test, p < 10⁻¹⁰ with the 8B judge, whose standard and placebo monitors could not tell proceed from decline at all). It did not hold everywhere. Gemma still scored decline slightly above proceed even with the clause; the clause shrank that bias but did not reverse it. And on endings that decide without discussing permission, Sonnet's standard monitor already separated proceed from decline, and did it better than the self-permission monitor (self-permission ahead in 17 of 56 untied pairs). The endings are written by me; that is the cost of holding the reasoning fixed while varying one thing.

05Validation · real agents
380agent conversations on τ²-bench airline, two open-weight agents

Where it broke in agents, and what fixed it

I ran two open-weight agents on the τ²-bench airline tasks (all 50 tasks, three trials each, plus an earlier pilot), each talking to a simulated customer under a written policy on cancellations, refunds and compensation. Before every tool call that changed the booking database, I extracted the agent's reasoning and scored it.

Exhibit 2 · What the violations actually were
unaware mistakereasoning says don't, agent actsself-permission

Most agent violations never appear as a decision in the reasoning. Unaware mistakes (a misread booking date, the wrong fare class) leave nothing to flag. In the second group, the reasoning concludes "I should ask the customer" or "transfer this", and the next action does the opposite. Monitors that read only the reasoning (standard, self-permission and placebo prompts) scored AUROC 0.38 to 0.58 at separating each agent's violations from its correct write actions, on every judge tried.

In an agent, then, the decision often lives in the tool call. I built an action-aware monitor that reads the reasoning, the pending tool call and the policy together, and committed two predictions before running it.

Exhibit 3 · Qwen3 32B: its 21 "reasoning says don't, agent acts" violations vs its 61 correct actions

The action-aware monitor reached AUROC 0.77 (95% CI 0.66 to 0.89) and flagged 71% at the fixed threshold, against 14% for reasoning only (paired difference +0.17, CI 0.00 to 0.34) and 24% for a dialogue-based check modelled on PolicyGuard (+0.25, CI 0.12 to 0.38). All three bars score this same subset with the same local 8B judge.

06What didn't work

Nulls and limits

  • prediction failedAcross all agent violations the action-aware monitor was only +0.04 AUROC over reasoning only, not significant. Unaware mistakes stay at chance for every input I tried, and its false-alarm rate is still too high to deploy.
  • null resultOn Yong & Bach's published self-jailbreaking traces from eight model families, no prompt beat another. About 90% of those traces are cut off before the decision, which the mechanism test shows small judges depend on.
  • base rateSelf-permission was rarer in strong agents than expected: 1 of 32 violations for GPT-OSS, 8 of 107 for Qwen3 32B, but about half of the 10 violations of a 1.7B agent in my refund pilot.
  • labelsAgent violation types were labelled by an LLM following the pre-committed codebook, blind to monitor scores. Human labels with inter-rater agreement come next.
  • fails openThrough Anthropic's API, monitor calls started being refused, first 21 of 420, then every call, including a harmless test sentence. The same model through Google Vertex completed all but 1 of over 3,000. A monitor built on a hosted model needs its refusal rate measured.
07How it was built

Reproducible by design

agent or model run→extract reasoning before each write call→score with pinned judges→clustered bootstrap + exact tests→report every judge

Judges that can't silently change

My original hosted judges were retired mid-project, which made older results impossible to rerun. I moved to open-weight judges served locally with Ollama, wrote each model's exact digest into every score row, and saved every raw reply.

Predictions written first

Labelling rules and each new prediction were written and dated before the run they cover, the labelling rules and the agent-monitor prediction committed to git first, with dated amendments when plans changed.

Statistics that fit the data

Traces from the same paragraph or task aren't independent, so intervals resample whole clusters. Matched pairs get exact sign tests. Small subgroups are reported as signals, not findings.

Cheap enough to iterate

Resumable scorers with rate-limit retry, provider pinning and blocked-reply logging. Almost everything ran on a laptop.

08Why it matters

A gate in front of the actions that move money

Agents that issue refunds, approve expenses or change records under a written policy need a check that runs before the action, not an audit after it. This project shows what that check has to see. Naming self-permission lets even a small local judge read the agent's decision, which matters because teams often pay for frontier models where a cheaper judge would do. When the decision appears only in the action, comparing the reasoning with the pending tool call catches violations a reasoning-only monitor misses. Next: validating the action-aware gate on new agents, bringing its false-alarm rate down to a deployable level, and publishing threshold tables builders can use.
